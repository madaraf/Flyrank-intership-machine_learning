Refresh Opportunity Queue: Ranking Declining Pages for Editorial Review

A content team can review a few dozen pages a week out of tens of thousands. This study asks which pages to look at first, and tests whether a learned model beats a simple, audited rule. Under a client-held-out evaluation, it does not reliably do so.

Abstract

Given a review budget far smaller than the portfolio, which declining pages should a content editor look at first? Using March–April 2026 data from a pseudonymized FlyRank warehouse (68,169 eligible pages across 34 clients), I defined a forward label (a 20% or larger impression drop) and compared an audited two-signal rule with Decision Tree, Random Forest and Gradient Boosting models under five-fold validation that holds out whole clients, scored by Precision@50. The rule scored 0.692 ± 0.155 against a base rate of 0.563; Gradient Boosting had the highest mean (0.724 ± 0.168), a gap smaller than either spread. Every learned model lost 0.14–0.20 AUC when clients were held out (the rule lost 0.005), and a candidate feature table with a suspected window overlap was excluded after its removal dropped AUC from 0.751 to 0.539, so the audited rule is the deployed result. It is a decision-support ranking with reason codes and human review, it makes no causal claim, and its absolute discrimination is weak (ROC AUC 0.588).

1Introduction and problem statement

Content decays. Rankings slip, clicks fall, and most teams notice late. The practical question is not “will this page decline?” in the abstract. It is an ordering problem: of everything that could be reviewed, what does a person open first?

  • Decision supported: the weekly review queue of a content editor or SEO strategist.
  • Unit of analysis: one content page, summarized over a feature window.
  • Task type: ranking. The output is an ordered queue with a reason code, not a yes/no label.
  • Success metric, fixed before modeling: Precision@50. Of the 50 pages ranked first, how many actually declined?
  • Cost of a wrong call: a false positive spends a scarce editor-hour on a page that did not need it; a false negative lets a real decline sit unreviewed.

2Data

Source. The pseudonymized FlyRank ML Internship warehouse release (daily search-performance facts, content metadata and a query-level table). Only hashed identifiers appear: no client names, domains, page URLs, titles or raw queries are used or shown anywhere in this paper.

Windows. Features come from March 2026 only. The label comes from April 2026, a separate, later month. Development used a mid-panel month and never the final month of the panel, which would leave no later window to predict into.

Eligibility. A page is eligible if it had at least 100 impressions in March and was at least 90 days old on March 31. This leaves 68,169 pages across 34 clients, out of 331,437 pages and 55 clients active in March (9,841,378 daily rows). The age floor removes pages still in their first-90-day ramp-up, where a 20% swing means something different from the same swing on an established page.

Label. is_declining_forward_label = 1 when April impressions are 20% or more below March impressions. Base rate: 0.563. Joining April onto the March-eligible pages dropped 0 of 68,169, so this construction shows no outcome-window survivorship.

3Methodology

Assumptions. This is an observational study of one portfolio. It describes association, not cause: nothing here shows that reviewing or refreshing a flagged page changes its future traffic.

Features, and when each is knowable

Five features are computed inside the March window, so each is knowable on March 31: impressions_month, ctr_month (clicks divided by impressions), avg_position_month, days_with_impressions, and content_age_days (the creation date is fixed in the past). The learned models also receive one engineered feature, age_risk_feature, the decline rate of the page’s age bucket, computed from training-fold rows only. That is the final six-feature list (SAFE_FEATURES), used everywhere in the study.

CTR falls steadily as position worsens. Staleness does not behave like a straight line: decline peaks at 91–180 days and is lowest for the oldest pages, so “older is always worse” is not supported here. The rule therefore uses a bucketed risk weight, not a linear age penalty.

The rule: score = 0.6 × (how far a page’s CTR falls below the typical CTR for its position bucket, clipped at zero and scaled) + 0.4 × (the decline rate of its age bucket, scaled). Both lookup tables are fit on training-fold rows only.

Validation design

Pages from one client share style, typical CTR and publishing cadence, and that fingerprint can reach a model through ordinary features even when the client ID itself is withheld. So every test fold contains only clients the model has never seen (GroupKFold, five folds, grouped by client). A naive random-row split is shown next to it, to measure how much that fingerprint inflates scores.

The tie rule matters. The rule clips the CTR gap at zero, so many pages share the top score. The deployed top ten all score 1.0 with a CTR of 0, and which tied pages fall inside the top 50 is decided by the tie rule, not by the score. Read the rule’s Precision@50 with that in mind.

Leakage checks

Check 1: a deliberate, obvious leak (proves the test can fail)

Adding the label’s own ingredient, percent change, as a feature moved ROC AUC from an honest 0.671 to 1.000. The column was then deleted, and it appears in no final feature list. A harness that cannot detect this cannot be trusted to detect anything subtler.

Check 2: a subtler candidate, tested rather than assumed

The query-level table offers three tempting features: how many queries a page shows for, and two impression-share fields. This repo’s data notes document that the table uses a fixed 90-day window that overlaps the snapshot’s final months and has no safe earlier-window variant. I did not query the table’s actual window dates, so overlap with April is strongly suspected, not verified.

The test: with the three features, grouped-CV AUC was 0.751; without them, 0.539, a drop of 0.212. That lands almost exactly on the original Random Forest’s own AUC (0.538). The query-count feature also showed a suspiciously clean pattern: decline rate fell 0.791, 0.612, 0.484, 0.391 across the four query-count buckets (0–1, 2–5, 6–20, 20+ queries; 15,955 / 15,261 / 21,314 / 15,639 pages), cleaner than either audited signal above. This is consistent with leakage, not proof of it. The three features were excluded conservatively and appear in no result below. They matched 85.1% of eligible pages; unmatched pages would have been filled with zero.

Check 3: does any single honest feature carry suspicious skill?

Dropping each of the five honest features in turn from a client-grouped Random Forest: removing ctr_month cost the most AUC (0.541 to 0.487), no removal collapsed the score toward 1.0, and removing content_age_days slightly raised it (0.541 to 0.583), a sign that raw age adds noise, in line with the MIXED staleness verdict. No gross leakage signature appears in the five honest features.

What the numbers say

  • No learned model reliably beats the rule. Gradient Boosting has the highest mean, but its 0.032 edge is smaller than either method’s spread. By fold it is higher than the rule in 3 of 5 folds and lower in 2.
  • The plain models do worse than the rule. The original Random Forest is higher than the rule in 2 of 5 folds (0.528 mean), and the original Decision Tree in 2 of 5 (0.656 mean).
  • Even the best result is modest. The rule’s Precision@50 is about 1.23× the base rate, and its ROC AUC of 0.588 is poor discrimination. It is the most trustworthy result here, not a strong one.

Why the learned models were excluded: the client-held-out gap

Under a naive random-row split the same models look far better: Decision Tree and Random Forest both reach Precision@50 of 1.00 (AUC 0.671 and 0.711), while the rule scores 0.72 (AUC 0.594). Holding out clients removes that advantage for the learned models and barely changes the rule. The gap between the two AUCs is therefore a direct measure of how much a model leans on client fingerprints it will not have for a new client.

How to read this exclusion honestly

The gap measures dependence on client identity, a failure to generalize. It is leakage-like, but it is not proof of label leakage. The 0.10 cutoff is a judgment call, set well below the 0.212 seen in the query-mix test and well above the rule’s 0.005; I chose it after seeing earlier runs, so it was not pre-registered. Each gap compares one random-split AUC with a five-fold mean, so it is itself noisy. The models were also given fixed hyperparameters, not tuned, so a tuned model could change this picture. The conclusion that survives all of these caveats is narrow: no learned model here showed it transfers to unseen clients better than the rule does.

5Limitations and honest framing

  1. One time step. A single feature month and a single outcome month, not a rolling backtest, and no sealed future month. Claims about other months are not supported.
  2. Large fold-to-fold spread. Precision@50 ranges from 0.24 to 0.88 for the original Random Forest across folds. Client sizes are uneven; in an earlier validation run the first fold’s 17,023 test rows came from a single client. No single fold is a stable estimate.
  3. Weak absolute performance. ROC AUC of 0.588 and a lift of about 1.23× over the base rate. Part of the rule’s Precision@50 comes from a block of tied top scores (all with CTR 0), which is a property of the rule, not evidence of ranking skill.
  4. Scope. Results describe the 68,169 eligible pages and the 34 clients with such pages, not the full portfolio or the 21 clients with no eligible page.
  5. Proxy label. A 20%+ impression drop is an observed pattern, not a diagnosis. A page can dip seasonally and recover untouched, or decline for reasons no refresh can fix.
  6. No causal claim. Nothing here shows that following the queue improves traffic. That would need a controlled experiment this project did not run.
  7. Query-mix exclusion is conservative, not proven (Section 3, Check 2). The table’s window dates were not queried.
  8. Engagement signals absent. GA4 fields were excluded for availability reasons, so engagement is not part of any ranking.
  9. Scores are not calibrated. They order pages; they are not probabilities of decline.
  10. One portfolio. Patterns may not transfer to a different content portfolio.

What I would test next

  1. Query the query-level table’s actual window dates, and rebuild its features on a window that ends before April.
  2. A rolling multi-month backtest with one sealed future month, evaluated once.
  3. Tuned learned models under the same client-held-out folds.
  4. Investigate the block of zero-CTR pages at the top of the queue, which may be templated or near-duplicate content.
  5. A controlled refresh experiment on flagged pages, to measure effect rather than ranking.

6Ranked recommendations: the action playbook

The deployed queue ranks all 68,169 eligible pages with the audited rule. Because the deployed method is the rule, the score and the explanation are one formula: each page carries a reason code, and the first ten pages all share the maximum score of 1.0. 7What this work demonstrates

Framing before modeling

A named actor, a fixed metric and a cost of error, set before any model was trained (Section 1).

A baseline worth beating

Two signals audited with bucket tables and sample sizes, one of them honestly MIXED (Section 3).

Tests that can fail

One obvious leak proved (0.671 to 1.000) and one subtle candidate tested and excluded (0.751 to 0.539).

Reporting a null result

No learned model reliably beat the rule under client-held-out folds, reported as the finding (Section 4).
